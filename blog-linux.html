Why I Switched to Linux as a Student

Aug 25, 2026 · 5 min read

I installed CachyOS on my laptop a few weeks ago, mostly out of curiosity and partly out of spite — I was tired of watching a background update eat 20% of my CPU for no reason I could see or control. A month in, it's still my daily driver, and I've learned a lot more about how my own computer works than I expected to.

I didn't pick Linux because I suddenly became a command-line wizard. I picked it because I'm the kind of person who wants to know why something works, not just that it works — and Windows is very good at hiding that "why" from you. Linux, for better and worse, does not hide anything.

Why CachyOS specifically

CachyOS is Arch-based, which meant two things going in: I'd get access to the Arch User Repository (basically an enormous library of community-maintained packages), and I'd have to actually read documentation instead of clicking "next" a few times. It's also built with performance as a first-class goal — optimized kernel, sensible defaults — which mattered to me since I didn't want to trade away the speed my laptop is supposed to have.

The installer was more hands-on than a typical Windows setup, but nowhere near as intimidating as vanilla Arch. It struck a fair middle ground for someone who wanted to learn, not just someone who wanted a working desktop by lunchtime.

What I like

  • Speed. Boot time, app launches, file operations — everything just feels lighter with fewer background processes competing for attention.
  • Package management that makes sense. Once pacman clicked for me, installing and updating software stopped feeling like a chore and started feeling like typing a sentence.
  • Actually understanding my system. Configuring my window manager, tweaking startup services, reading logs when something breaks — it's taught me more about operating systems in a month than any textbook chapter has.
  • The terminal stopped being scary. I now reach for it before I reach for a GUI, which felt impossible three months ago.

What I don't like

  • The learning curve is real. Windows tells you what went wrong in plain English (sometimes). Linux tells you in a log file you have to go find yourself.
  • Rolling release means occasional surprises. An update once left me without working Wi-Fi for an evening. It was fixable in ten minutes once I found the right forum post, but that ten minutes required knowing where to look.
  • A few college-required apps just don't have a clean Linux version. Nothing dramatic — mostly formatting quirks when submitting certain documents — but enough that I still keep a small Windows partition around for emergencies.
The biggest shift wasn't technical, it was psychological — I stopped treating my laptop as a sealed box and started treating it as something I'm allowed to understand.

Would I recommend it to every first-year engineering student? Only if you're genuinely curious about the "why," not just looking for a free upgrade. If you want a system that stays out of your way, stick with what you have. If you want a system that occasionally makes you Google things at 11 PM and then teaches you something real in the process — that's exactly what this has been for me.
